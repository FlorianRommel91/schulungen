Staying Safe at Sicuralife

Swiss law asks every employer — even a company of three — to walk the team through workplace safety once a year. This isn't about hard hats and machinery guards. It's about the desk you sit at all day, the boxes you lift in Contone, and knowing exactly what to do if something goes wrong. About fifteen minutes, once a year, so nobody gets hurt doing ordinary work.

Module 1

Why This Matters — and Who's Responsible

Sicuralife makes a product that keeps people safe. It would be a strange gap if we didn't do the same for the people who build, configure, and ship it.

⚖️
Legal basis: the Swiss Labour Act (ArG) and EKAS Guideline 6508 require every employer — regardless of size — to instruct staff on workplace hazards and safe behaviour, and to repeat that instruction regularly. That's exactly what "Occupational Safety (annual instruction)" in the QF06 Training Matrix covers.
🧑‍💼
Employer's duty (Jérôme)

Provide a safe workplace and equipment, identify hazards, and make sure everyone gets this instruction — repeated at least once a year.

🙋
Your duty

Follow safe practices, use equipment as intended, and report anything that looks unsafe — a loose cable, a blocked exit, a way of working that feels risky.

Quick check: myth or fact?

"Occupational safety rules are mainly for factories and construction sites, not a small software/hardware company like us."
Myth. Swiss law applies to every employer, however small — desks and warehouses have their own hazards too.
"This instruction has to happen at least once a year, not just once when you're hired."
Fact. EKAS 6508 asks for regular, repeated instruction — habits fade if they're never refreshed.
"Reporting a hazard you spotted — even if nothing happened yet — is part of your job, not extra effort."
Fact. Catching a hazard before it causes an accident is exactly the point.
"Safety is only really Jérôme's problem to think about."
Myth. The employer sets it up, but everyday safety depends on everyone following it and speaking up.

Quick scenario check

SituationYou notice a power strip in the Contone warehouse overloaded with adapters and getting warm to the touch. What's the right move?
A warm, overloaded power strip is exactly the kind of early warning sign that shouldn't wait.
Exactly — flag it immediately. A small fix now beats a fire risk later.

Module 2

Screen & Office Ergonomics

Most of the working day at Sicuralife happens in front of a screen — development, support, sales, admin. The most common "workplace injury" here isn't dramatic, it's a stiff neck or sore wrists that build up slowly.

🪑
Rule of thumb: screen top at eye level, elbows roughly at 90°, feet flat on the floor. Small adjustments, made once, prevent most of the discomfort.

Do

  • Take a short break and look away from the screen every 20–30 minutes
  • Adjust chair and screen height instead of hunching to fit them
  • Stand up and move around over lunch or between calls

Don't

  • Work for hours on a laptop screen without an external monitor or stand
  • Ignore recurring wrist, neck or eye strain — it tends to get worse, not better, on its own
  • Sit in the same position for an entire working day

The 20-20-20 habit

Every 20 minutes

Take a moment away from the screen.

Look 20 feet away

Roughly 6 metres — long enough to let your eyes refocus.

For 20 seconds

That's enough. Then get back to it.

Quick scenario check

SituationYou've been getting a sore neck most afternoons for the past two weeks, working from a laptop screen on the kitchen table.
A pattern like this over two weeks is your body telling you the setup, not the day, is the problem.
Exactly — fix the setup first. Small ergonomic changes usually solve it before it becomes a real issue.

Manual handling & general hazards

📦
Lifting boxes

Bend your knees, not your back, for anything heavy. If a box feels too heavy to lift alone, don't force it — ask for help.

🔋
Lithium batteries

Handle devices and batteries gently — no dropping, puncturing, or exposing to heat. A damaged or swollen battery goes aside, never on charge or in with the good stock. (Shipping rules for lithium batteries are covered separately in the IATA training.)

🔌
Electrical safety

Don't overload power strips, don't leave chargers running unattended overnight, and check cables for damage before use.

🚪
Keep walkways clear

Boxes and pallets never block the door or a walking path — that's both a trip hazard and a blocked escape route.

Fire safety basics

🧯
Know where the fire extinguisher is before you need it, not while something's burning. If in doubt where it is in Contone, ask Lisa on your next visit — and check it again during this year's instruction.

Quick check: safe or unsafe?

A box of packaging material is stacked neatly against the wall, not blocking anything.
Fine. Stacked neatly and out of the way — no hazard here.
Three extension cords are daisy-chained together to reach a workstation.
Unsafe. Daisy-chaining extension cords is a common cause of overheating and electrical fires.
A pallet of boxes is left in front of the warehouse door overnight.
Unsafe. That's exactly the blocked-exit situation to avoid — move it clear of the door.

Quick scenario check

SituationWhile unboxing a returned unit, you notice the battery looks swollen and slightly warm.
Charging a swollen battery is one of the riskier things you could do with it — never do that.
Exactly — isolate it, don't charge or damage it further, and let Jérôme or Lisa know.

Module 4

Emergencies, First Aid & Reporting

Most days nothing happens. This module is about the rare day when something does — so nobody has to figure it out on the spot.

Swiss emergency numbers

NumberFor
144Ambulance / medical emergency
118Fire brigade
117Police
112General emergency (works EU-wide, including from a mobile)

If someone is injured

Stop & assess

Make sure the area is safe before approaching — don't create a second casualty.

Call for help if needed

144 for anything beyond a minor scrape. Don't hesitate if unsure — better an unnecessary call than a delay.

Tell Jérôme or Lisa

Even a minor injury gets reported, not just the serious ones.

If there's a fire

Get out first

Leave the building before trying to fight anything beyond a very small, contained fire.

Call 118

From a safe location, once you're out.

Don't go back in

Not for stock, not for a laptop. Nothing in the warehouse is worth that risk.

📋
Report near-misses too — not just actual accidents. If a box almost fell on someone, or a cable almost tripped someone, that's exactly the kind of early signal that prevents the real accident next time. Tell Jérôme or Lisa directly; there's no need to file it anywhere formal.

Quick scenario check

SituationA colleague slips on a wet floor near the warehouse entrance but isn't hurt — just startled. What now?
No injury doesn't mean no lesson — the same spot could catch someone else next time.
Exactly — a near-miss is free information. Flag it and fix the cause if you can.

Five Rules to Remember

  1. This instruction is a legal requirement, repeated every year — not a one-off.
  2. Set your screen up properly and take a real break every 20–30 minutes.
  3. Lift with your knees, never force a heavy lift alone, and treat batteries gently.
  4. Keep walkways and exits clear, and know where the fire extinguisher is.
  5. Report injuries, near-misses, and anything that looks unsafe — every time, not just the big ones.
